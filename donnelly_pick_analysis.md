## Pick-by-Pick Breakdown

### 1. Crazy Cavalier — Santa Anita R5, March 15, 2026 (NHC Final Table)

**Race details:** 1 mile on turf, Santa Anita Park. Allowance optional claiming, $70,000 purse. 8 starters (Sir Percival scratched from 9 entries). Final time 1:35.03. Fractions: :22.7, :46.92, 1:10.91, 1:22.89.

**Running line:** Unhurried early in the two path. Advanced three then four wide on the far turn. Rallied from outside, fought five deep into the last sixteenth, proved best late. Won by approximately 1 length over None Above the Law.

**Odds:** 10-1 morning line, 10-1 at post. The favorites were Baldoro (6-1 ML) and Beef Winslow (2-1 ML, who ran 4th).

**Likely rationale:** Donnelly identified this race as a pace-dependent scenario. His own words confirm the analysis: "I thought the one (Kokosan) would be tough, but if there was pace, the four (Crazy Cavalier) was going to be the best closer. He was in form." The fractions support the read — :22.7 and :46.92 for the first half-mile on Santa Anita's turf course suggest a pace that was at least honest, setting up closers. The running line confirms Crazy Cavalier was unhurried early and rallied from well off the pace, exactly the profile Donnelly described targeting.

**Dossier alignment:** Near-perfect. This pick illustrates all three of Donnelly's stated filters: (1) the horse was "in form" — proven fast enough recently; (2) the pace scenario favored closers; (3) the one-mile turf distance was within the horse's proven range. The 10-1 odds are consistent with the dossier's characterization that Donnelly's overlays come not from deliberate longshot-hunting but from backing proven horses the crowd is undervaluing. The game-theoretic dimension is also visible: from 4th place at the Final Table, he correctly anticipated that leaders would play shorter-priced horses and took the bigger swing.

**Speed figure note:** Horse Racing Nation assigned a 113 figure to Crazy Cavalier's win. This is not a Beyer; it is HRN's proprietary figure. Without pre-race figures, we cannot reconstruct whether Donnelly's own adjusted figures flagged this horse as competitive. But the 10-1 odds suggest the public did not view the horse as the most likely winner, which is exactly the kind of disagreement Donnelly's replay-adjusted figures are designed to exploit.

### 2. British Isles — Santa Anita R10 (Santa Anita Handicap G1), March 7, 2026

**Race details:** 1¼ miles on dirt, fast track. Grade I, $300,000 purse. 5 starters (Skippylongstocking and Westwood scratched from 7 entries). Final time 2:05.17. Fractions: :22.86, :46.63, 1:11.31.

**Running line:** Stalked the top pair (Midnight Mammoth led, Getaway Car second) from off the rail in 3rd position early. Inched closer three wide on the backstretch. Challenged three deep between rivals at the quarter pole, seized command shortly after, kicked clear in upper stretch, extended the margin under right-handed encouragement. Won by 4½ lengths.

**Odds:** 6-1 morning line, 7.20-1 at post. Just a Touch was the 3-5 favorite.

**Likely rationale:** Donnelly articulated this pick publicly before the race, making it the most transparent window into his process. His case rested on distance affinity — his third filter — applied with unusual conviction: "He was the only one who had shown he could go that far, and that's massive." British Isles had strong turf form at 1½ and 1⅜ miles. When critics pointed to a 5th-place Pegasus World Cup finish at Gulfstream, Donnelly's trip notes identified the excuse: "I upgraded his Pegasus a lot because he was running on the inside and the inside was not where you wanted to be." He saw through a misleading result to proven distance capability that the field didn't match.

**Dossier alignment:** This is the textbook illustration of Donnelly's "dirtied-up form" thesis. A horse with proven ability whose recent result looked bad because of a track bias (inside lane at Gulfstream) was trading at inflated odds. The public saw 5th in the Pegasus; Donnelly saw a horse with unique distance credentials whose bad race was explainable. The 7-1 odds in a 5-horse Grade I field — where the favorite was 3-5 — reflect a market that had essentially written off British Isles. Donnelly's willingness to back a horse switching from turf routes to a 10-furlong dirt stakes is notable, though the dossier's emphasis on "proven form" could be read as cutting either way here: the horse had proven the distance on turf but was being asked to replicate it on dirt.

**Pace context:** The fractions (:22.86, :46.63, 1:11.31) describe a moderate pace for a 10-furlong dirt race. British Isles sat a stalking trip in 3rd — not a deep closer like Crazy Cavalier, but a horse positioned off the pace and able to pounce when ready. The 4½-length margin suggests this wasn't pace-dependent in the same way as the Crazy Cavalier pick; British Isles was simply the best horse in a weakened field, and Donnelly's edge was recognizing that fact when the market didn't.

## Pattern Analysis

Of the 6 documented horse-level picks:
- 10-1: Crazy Cavalier, I'm Buzzy
- 9-1: Hallie's Hero, Abbaa
- 7-1: British Isles
- ~2.4-1: Irish Heatwave

Five of six picks were at 7-1 or higher. The lone exception (Irish Heatwave at 2.4-1) came in a final-race tournament situation where Donnelly was closing and needed to cash, not bomb. The median is approximately 9-1. This is a price range that produces overlays without requiring extreme longshot variance — consistent with the dossier's characterization that Donnelly's prices are a byproduct of his dirtied-up-form thesis rather than deliberate longshot targeting.

### Running style

For the two picks with running line data:
- **Crazy Cavalier:** Deep closer. Unhurried early, rallied from behind, won going away.
- **British Isles:** Stalker/presser. Sat 3rd early, moved three wide, seized command mid-stretch.

These are different running styles, but both were selected in the context of specific pace reads. Crazy Cavalier was a closer picked because "if there was pace" he'd be the best finisher. British Isles was a stalker picked because he had unique distance credentials. Donnelly is not wedded to one running style — he picks the style that fits the pace scenario, which is exactly what his framework predicts.

- Crazy Cavalier: Allowance optional claiming
- I'm Buzzy: Claiming
- Irish Heatwave: Allowance (NW1$X)

The range is wide — from Grade I to claiming. This is consistent with contest play, where mandatory races span all class levels. No class-level concentration is evident, though the two most detailed picks (Crazy Cavalier, British Isles) are at mid-to-upper class levels.

### Track concentration

Santa Anita appears in 2 of 6 picks. Golden Gate Fields and Woodbine appear once each, as does Belmont. Donnelly's Southern California base and his documented history of attending Santa Anita since childhood make the SA concentration unsurprising. The dossier notes he watches replays at "specific tracks" — Santa Anita is almost certainly one of his primary replay tracks, giving him a trip-note edge there.

The dossier describes 2020 as his breakout year — "full speed ahead" — and these numbers support that. However, these are self-reported via HorseTourneys blogs and only surface on days he won, introducing survivorship bias.

## Dossier Validation

### What the pick record confirms

1. **Pace analysis as a selection driver, not just a filter.** Both fully documented picks show Donnelly reading pace scenarios and selecting the running style best positioned to exploit them. Crazy Cavalier was a closer in a pace scenario; British Isles was a stalker with unique distance stamina in a moderate-pace route. The framework isn't "pick closers" or "pick pressers" — it's "read the pace, pick the style that benefits."

2. **The dirtied-up-form thesis in practice.** British Isles is the cleanest example: a horse with proven distance ability whose Pegasus World Cup 5th was explained away by inside-path bias at Gulfstream. The public discounted the horse; Donnelly upgraded. This is exactly the mechanism the dossier describes.

3. **Distance affinity as a genuine differentiator.** British Isles was selected specifically because "he was the only one who had shown he could go that far." Donnelly didn't just prefer proven distance — he used it as the primary basis for separation in a Grade I stakes.

4. **Game-theoretic tournament positioning.** The Crazy Cavalier selection was simultaneously a handicapping pick and a tournament move. From 4th at the Final Table, Donnelly correctly read that leaders would play defensively, and he took the contrarian swing. The dossier's description of his positional awareness is validated.

5. **Moderate-to-high odds range as a byproduct, not a target.** The 7-1 to 10-1 cluster fits the dossier's "overlay from dirtied-up form" thesis. These aren't 30-1 bombs or 2-1 chalk — they're the natural price range of proven horses whose recent form looks worse than it is.

### What the picks reveal beyond the dossier

1. **Turf affinity.** Two of three documented route picks are on turf (Crazy Cavalier, Irish Heatwave), and the turf-to-dirt crossover (British Isles) was itself keyed on turf distance credentials. The dossier never mentions turf as a preference, but the pick record leans toward it. This may reflect Santa Anita's turf course being a primary replay-watching venue for Donnelly, or it may be sample noise.

2. **Southern California track edge.** Both fully documented picks are at Santa Anita. The dossier mentions his grandfather took him to Santa Anita as a child and that he handicaps by watching "every race at a specific track." The picks suggest Santa Anita is likely his highest-confidence venue — the one where trip notes are deepest and figure adjustments most reliable.

3. **Late-contest acceleration.** The Woodbine Exacta Extravaganza comeback (scoreless for 15 races, then 6 of 12 to close) and the general pattern of "starts slow, finishes strong" described in the HorseTourneys blogs suggest something the dossier doesn't explicitly name: Donnelly may be deliberately conservative early in multi-day contests, building information through the card before committing. Alternatively, his replay-intensive approach may produce better reads as a card develops and pace biases become visible.

4. **Format flexibility in selection criteria.** The I'm Buzzy pick (6-furlong dirt claimer on a sloppy track) and the Woodbine exacta play (two 9-1 shots) suggest that Donnelly's selection criteria shift with contest format. In NHC championship play, he appears to lean into his core framework (proven form, pace, distance). In HorseTourneys feeders and exotic-format contests, he may widen his selection aperture — willing to play sprints, claimers, and off-track conditions that don't appear in his championship picks. This is logical: feeder contests reward volume and aggression differently than Final Tables.

### What the picks cannot confirm or deny

- **His specific commercial figure service.** No pick provides evidence for or against any named product.
- **His figure adjustment methodology.** We know he adjusts figures manually from replays, but the picks don't reveal the magnitude or direction of his adjustments.
- **How he handles maiden or 2-year-old races.** No documented picks involve maidens or juveniles.
- **His approach to sprints as a systematic matter.** One sprint pick in a feeder is insufficient to characterize a sprint methodology.

## Data Limitations and Caveats

### The sample problem

Six named-horse picks across six years is not a meaningful statistical sample. Any "pattern" identified from six data points is one or two picks away from reversal. The odds profile, surface lean, and running-style observations above should be treated as directional signals, not established tendencies.

### Survivorship and selection bias

The picks that survive into the public record are disproportionately winners. Donnelly's losing picks in the NHC Day 1 and Day 2 mandatory races — the bulk of his tournament selections — are not published anywhere. Media coverage naturally highlights the winning pick (Crazy Cavalier) and the pre-tournament call that hit (British Isles). His HorseTourneys picks surface on days he won contests, not days he lost. This means the documented record systematically overrepresents his successes and tells us nothing about his failure modes.

### The missing middle of the process

We have Donnelly's stated inputs (replays, trip notes, adjusted figures, pace reads, distance affinity) and his outputs (specific horse selections). What we lack is the middle: how he weights conflicting signals, what happens when his figure top pick is a bad pace fit, how he resolves ties between candidates, how he approaches races where no horse meets all three of his filters. This middle is where the actual edge lives, and it is entirely unobservable from public data.

### Equibase inaccessibility

Equibase — the primary source for detailed running lines, fractional splits, chart comments, and field-level data — was inaccessible due to Incapsula bot protection. This is not a paywall issue; the data is nominally public but technically blocked. A researcher with browser access to Equibase could substantially enrich the 4 named picks that lack running line data (Irish Heatwave, Hallie's Hero, Abbaa, I'm Buzzy), potentially adding fractional times, running positions, margin details, and chart-caller trip notes.

### What a larger sample would change

If full NHC Day 1, Day 2, and Semifinal picks for 2025 and 2026 were available (approximately 28 mandatory-race selections), the analysis would shift from anecdote to statistical characterization. We could assess: what percentage of his picks are closers vs. stalkers vs. speed; what his actual strike rate is across all plays (not just winning-day plays); whether his odds distribution genuinely clusters at 7-1 to 10-1 or includes substantial chalk play that doesn't make the highlight reel; and whether the turf lean holds up or is an artifact of two Santa Anita picks. Without that data, this analysis describes six moments in a career, not a methodology in operation.
